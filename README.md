# VPS pricing: what you actually pay, what drives the bill, and where LisaHost fits

Searching for **VPS pricing** usually sounds simpler than it is. A host can advertise a server at one monthly number, while another reaches a similar headline price through a longer prepaid term, a different traffic allowance, shared CPU, or a very different IP/network setup.

The current 2026 market makes that especially obvious. Third-party pricing surveys published this year put ordinary entry-level VPS offers anywhere from roughly **$3 to $8 per month**, mainstream 4–8 GB machines around **$10 to $35 per month**, and dedicated-core or memory-heavy systems substantially higher. Another current comparison puts a typical 2 vCPU / 4 GB VPS anywhere from roughly $4 to $40 per month depending on provider, storage, network and billing model.

LisaHost sits in a different part of that spectrum. Its public catalog is heavily oriented toward **native-IP, dual-ISP residential-IP, optimized-route and VDS products**, so a direct price comparison with a generic cloud VPS is only useful after you decide what kind of IP, traffic model and network route you actually need. Its current cart is divided into separate product families rather than one universal pricing grid.

The figures below are a current snapshot checked against LisaHost’s public pages on **September 28, 2026**. Prices are shown in the currency LisaHost displays, mainly CNY. Where the site marks a price as a special or limited-time price, I keep that distinction instead of presenting it as a normal undiscounted list price.

## What is a normal VPS price in 2026?

There is no single “normal” VPS price because VPS products are sold under several different billing and infrastructure models.

For a basic unmanaged Linux VPS, current market comparisons put entry pricing in the low single digits to high single digits per month. Once you move into 4 GB RAM machines, monthly pricing commonly rises into the low double digits, while providers charging for more dedicated CPU resources, management or larger memory footprints can go much higher.

A second issue is billing. A plan advertised as “$6.49/month” can mean a long prepaid introductory term rather than a genuinely month-to-month $6.49 purchase. Current 2026 VPS comparisons explicitly warn that first-term pricing, monthly billing and renewal pricing can differ substantially.

That distinction matters with LisaHost too, although its model is different from the common Western cloud-hosting promotion model. Most of its current product pages display the actual billing period directly as **monthly, quarterly or yearly**, and some special annual offers are dramatically lower on an effective monthly basis.

So the right question is not simply “How much is a VPS?”

It is:

> **How much does the exact CPU/RAM/storage/network/IP/traffic combination I need cost on the billing period I can actually accept?**

That is the number worth comparing.

## Monthly vs quarterly vs yearly: compare the total, not the headline

This is one of the biggest traps in VPS pricing.

A monthly price answers “what does it cost to keep this server for one month?”

A yearly price answers “what do I pay upfront to reserve it for a year?”

Those are not interchangeable.

LisaHost currently publishes several annual products around the **¥399–¥899/year** range, including US, Singapore, Taiwan, UK, Japan, Germany and residential-IP products.

The effective monthly cost can look attractive, but the trade-off is obvious: you have paid the full annual amount upfront. That matters more when you're unsure about the route, IP reputation, workload or long-term need.

There is also a real difference between LisaHost’s monthly residential plans and its special annual offers. The ¥499/year US 9929 residential offer has 1 CPU, 1 GB RAM, 10 GB NVMe, 50 Mbps bandwidth and 600 GB monthly traffic. The standard ¥68/month 9929 Lite has the same CPU and RAM but 10 GB NVMe, 50 Mbps bandwidth and **1,000 GB monthly traffic**.

So the annual offer is cheaper on the cash outlay basis, but it is not identical.

That is exactly the kind of detail that gets lost in a “VPS pricing from ¥41/month” headline.

## The biggest pricing question: datacenter IP or residential IP?

LisaHost's catalog makes this distinction unusually prominent.

Its US 9929 and 4837 lines advertise dual-ISP residential-IP configurations, while its home-broadband VDS products explicitly describe hardware installed on residential broadband connections. The latter are priced considerably higher than the entry-level optimized VPS products.

This is where a generic VPS pricing comparison can become misleading.

A standard cloud VPS can be perfectly appropriate for:

* a small web server;
* a development environment;
* Docker workloads;
* an internal application;
* a private service;
* lightweight APIs.

A residential-IP VPS solves a different problem. The reason to pay more is usually the **IP/network characteristic**, not another 1 GB of RAM.

The same logic explains why a LisaHost residential VDS can cost hundreds or more per month while a standard 1 GB VPS can cost tens of yuan. You are buying a different infrastructure and IP model. The official pages also impose special-use restrictions on some high-quality residential-IP products, including prohibitions on spam, mass mail, attacks, scanning, phishing and resource abuse. Violations can trigger immediate suspension without refund.

That limitation matters more than a few extra CPU cores when evaluating the true price.

## Bandwidth and traffic can completely change the comparison

The current LisaHost catalog contains several examples where RAM changes modestly but traffic and bandwidth change sharply.

The US New York and Chicago residential plans move from **300 Mbps / 3,000 GB** at ¥68 to **500 Mbps / 8,000 GB** at ¥100, and then to **1 Gbps / 20,000 GB** at ¥300. The separate unlimited Lite products trade down to 200 Mbps while removing the monthly traffic cap, at ¥198.

That is a useful illustration of why comparing only vCPU and RAM is incomplete.

Suppose two providers both give you 2 vCPU and 2 GB RAM. One may cap traffic at a few hundred gigabytes, another may include several terabytes, and a third may bill by usage. On an always-on service, those differences can matter more than the CPU number.

Current industry pricing guides make the same point: VPS cost differences can come from storage class, shared-core policy, network capacity, transfer limits, IPv4 charges, support and billing terms, even when the headline specifications look similar.

## What the LisaHost network labels actually tell you

The current catalog repeatedly uses labels such as **CN2 GIA, AS9929, AS4837, CMI and BGP**.

Those labels are meaningful for users whose traffic path matters, but they are not interchangeable marketing adjectives.

For example, LisaHost's Hong Kong product specifically describes CMI/CU2/CN2 direct connectivity, while the Singapore product is presented as a BGP international route and explicitly notes that it is **not optimized for direct mainland-China access**. The Japanese native-IP product is presented as a mainland-optimized international route, while the UK, Singapore and some residential products recommend using an intermediary route for certain mainland users.

That means a ¥68 Singapore VPS and a ¥68 US or Germany VPS do not represent the same network experience.

The practical way to compare them is to start with your users:

**Users in mainland China:** route characteristics and return paths can be more important than nominal bandwidth.

**Users mainly in North America:** New York, Chicago, Los Angeles and Seattle offer materially different geographic placement and network characteristics.

**Japan-specific use:** the current catalog separates native-IP VPS from IIJ residential-IP VDS products, with very different pricing.

**Regional-IP requirements:** a “native IP” product can justify a higher price even when the server resources themselves look ordinary.

That is why “cheapest VPS” is a weak search criterion on its own.

## How LisaHost pricing compares with the wider market

There is a simple reason the LisaHost numbers do not line up neatly with the mainstream cloud-provider comparisons you see in English-language search results.

Mainstream cloud VPS pricing is often built around generalized compute: vCPU, RAM, storage and transfer. LisaHost's current catalog is much more segmented around **route quality, regional IP type, residential IP characteristics, high-bandwidth variants and VDS offerings**.

A current market comparison, for example, shows entry-level generic VPS plans around the $4–$5 range for some providers and much higher prices for larger or more flexible instances.

That does not mean a ¥68 LisaHost plan is “cheaper” or “more expensive” in any useful universal sense. It means the products are solving different problems.

A better apples-to-apples comparison is:

1. same RAM;
2. same approximate CPU capacity;
3. same storage technology;
4. same traffic allowance;
